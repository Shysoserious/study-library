Recap: Feasible and Optimal Solutions
Feasible set
Ω= {x ∈Rn : gi(x) ≤0 for all i, hj(x) = 0 for all j}.
▶A feasible point is any x ∈Ω.
▶An optimal solution x⋆is a feasible point with
f(x⋆) ≤f(x)
for every x ∈Ω.
▶The optimal value is the number f(x⋆). Several solutions may tie,
but they share the same value.
▶A local minimizer is best among nearby feasible points. A global
minimizer is best over all of Ω.

Diagnostic Checklist
Before choosing an algorithm, ask these questions in order:
1. Feasible? Does at least one decision satisfy every constraint?
2. Bounded? Is the objective bounded in the direction of optimization?
3. Attained? Does a feasible point reach the best finite bound?
4. Unique? Is there one optimal solution, or do several solutions tie?
▶Constraints determine where we may search.
▶The objective determines which feasible decisions are better.
▶The model itself determines whether a best solution exists.

Linear and Nonlinear Problems
Linear optimization (LP) has a linear objective and linear constraints.
Nonlinear optimization (NLP) has at least one nonlinear expression.
▶Linear inequalities define half-spaces; linear equalities define
hyperplanes. Their intersection is a polyhedron.
▶A linear objective has parallel level lines. On a bounded polygon, at
least one optimal corner exists; an entire edge may tie.
▶Nonlinear models may have curved boundaries or curved level sets.

Classify a model along three independent dimensions:

▶unconstrained or constrained
2. Formula shape
▶linear or nonlinear
3. Variable type
▶continuous, pure integer, or mixed-integer.

same time. The labels answer different questions and do not compete.
Course convention: unless stated otherwise, decision variables are

Classification Depends on Mathematical Form
The same decision problem can sometimes be written in different
mathematical forms. The formulation affects which algorithms we can use.
▶An exact reformulation changes the mathematical form but preserves
the optimal value and lets us recover an optimal decision for the
original problem.
▶A continuous relaxation removes an integer restriction. It allows more
decisions, so it may produce a better objective value that the original
integer model cannot attain.
Why this matters
A useful reformulation may expose linear structure and make a model easier
to solve. A relaxation provides a bound, but it is not automatically an exact
replacement.

Why Modeling Matters
A real problem usually arrives as a sentence:
“How much of each alloy should the factory produce?”
The sentence leaves important details unspecified.
▶Decision: What quantities can the factory choose?
▶Objective: Does “best” mean more profit, lower cost, or less waste?
▶Constraints: Which resources and operating rules limit the choices?
The role of a model
A mathematical model makes the choices, goal, and assumptions visible
before a solver searches for an answer.
A useful model is correct, clear, solvable, and robust. Small data errors
should not produce unreasonable decisions.

A Practical Modeling Pipeline
1. Clarify the goal. For Production, maximize total profit rather than
simply producing more.
2. Choose decision variables. Let x1 and x2 be the numbers of
production units of Alloy 1 and Alloy 2.
3. Translate the rules. Use the steel, iron, and copper inventories to
limit x1 and x2.
4. Check the model. The plan (0, 0) is feasible. The plan (100, 100) is
not, because it needs 200 units of copper.
5. Improve the formulation when needed. Simplify only when the
new formulation keeps the same meaning and allowed decisions.
A good working habit
Start with plain language. Introduce mathematics only after every symbol
has a concrete meaning.

Common Modeling Mistakes
1. Poor numerical scaling
▶A cost variable may reach millions of dollars, while a time variable
may be as small as 0.01 seconds, which equals 10 milliseconds.
▶Expressing cost in thousands of dollars or time in milliseconds can
give the model more comparable numerical scales.
2. Assuming that a helper variable makes a model linear
▶To obtain an LP, helper variables must be linked to the original
variables by linear constraints.
▶Convex piecewise-linear functions often have linear formulations.
Nonconvex piecewise-linear rules may require binary variables.
3. Omitting a real operating rule
▶A production model may assume unlimited sales unless it includes a
demand limit.
Reformulation check
The new model must preserve the optimal value and let us recover an optimal
decision for the original problem.
